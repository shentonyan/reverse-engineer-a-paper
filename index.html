Reverse-Engineer a Paper

A paper is a story wrapped around data.

We tend to treat a paper as a container for information. Readers need to be led, sentence by sentence, and every sentence should have a job the reader can see.

Scroll to take one apart, then try a few small exercises.

An independent explainer in original wording, inspired by the reverse-engineering approach in Hilary Glasman-Deal's Science Research Writing (2nd ed.). Not a reproduction of the book.

A paper is made of sentences

Every article you have read is a long run of sentences in a set order. Each square here stands for one.

Illustration, not real data.

Each part has a job

From top to bottom: introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion. Readers expect this order, so the order itself carries meaning.

Zoom in on one introduction

Six sentences from an invented introduction. Each does a different job, and together they walk the reader toward what the author did.

A paper has a shape

An introduction narrows from a wide context to one gap. Results are the narrowest point. The discussion opens back out. Title and abstract sit on top as a preview.

Copy the method, not the text

Model: map the job of each sentence in a good paper. Mine: collect the phrases that do each job. Master: the grammar choices that change meaning, such as tense. Update: journals change, so keep resampling.

Now your own paper

Pick five recent papers from your target journal. Label every sentence of one section with its job. Copy the phrases that do each job into your own list. Three exercises below get you started.
